**Chai Discovery raised $400 million in a Series C round at a $3.8 billion valuation**, announced July 19, 2026 and led by **Index Ventures**, with **Kleiner Perkins, Sequoia and Dimension** co-leading. The raise nearly triples the company's valuation from just seven months earlier, underscoring how much capital is still flowing into AI-driven drug discovery.

Here's the full breakdown of the round, the backers, and what it means for AI in pharma.

## What is Chai Discovery?

Chai Discovery builds **AI models for molecular design and drug discovery**. Founded in **2020**, the company uses computational design to predict and optimize drug candidates, aiming for better binding affinity and higher target success rates than traditional discovery methods.

In plain terms: finding a molecule that binds effectively to a biological target is normally a slow, expensive process of lab experimentation and trial and error. Chai Discovery's models are built to predict which candidate molecules are most likely to work before they ever reach the lab, compressing an expensive part of the drug pipeline. Its customers include **Pfizer** and **Eli Lilly**.

## The raise: $400M Series C

The headline numbers:

- **Amount:** $400 million
- **Round:** Series C
- **Valuation:** $3.8 billion
- **Announced:** July 19, 2026
- **Lead investor:** Index Ventures
- **Co-leads:** Kleiner Perkins, Sequoia, Dimension

Chai Discovery's Series B was $1.3 billion in valuation, raised in December 2025. Going from a $1.3 billion valuation to $3.8 billion in about seven months is nearly a **3x increase**, one of the sharper valuation climbs among AI biotech companies this year.

## Who invested in Chai Discovery?

The Series C was led by **Index Ventures**, with **Kleiner Perkins**, **Sequoia** and **Dimension** co-leading the round.

The investor lineup blends generalist growth firms (Sequoia, Index, Kleiner Perkins) with Dimension, a specialist life-sciences investor — a combination that suggests both broad AI conviction and domain-specific confidence in Chai's approach to drug discovery.

## What Chai Discovery will do with the money

The company says the funding will go toward **compute resources, hiring, and go-to-market expansion in enterprise pharma**. Scaling molecular design models requires significant compute for training and inference, and expanding enterprise partnerships with pharmaceutical companies means building out sales and support teams that can work within pharma's long, regulated development cycles.

## Why it matters

1. **AI-for-biology remains one of the most heavily funded corners of the AI boom.** Chai's valuation nearly tripling in seven months shows investors still believe computational design can meaningfully compress the drug discovery pipeline, even as funding cools in some other AI categories. Drug discovery has always been an attractive target for AI because the traditional process is so slow and expensive — even modest improvements in candidate success rates translate into enormous savings across a pharmaceutical company's pipeline.
2. **Enterprise adoption from Pfizer and Eli Lilly signals real production use.** Unlike many AI startups still in pilot phases, Chai has paying relationships with two of the largest pharmaceutical companies in the world — evidence that its models are being used in actual drug development work, not just evaluated. That distinction matters in biotech AI specifically, where a long list of startups have struggled to convert pharma pilots into recurring, revenue-generating partnerships.
3. **The round adds to a broader wave of mega-rounds tracked across 2026**, joining a growing list of AI companies posting steep valuation jumps within a single year, as cataloged in [the biggest AI funding rounds of 2026](/blog/biggest-ai-funding-rounds-2026).

Chai's next test will be whether its computationally designed candidates actually clear the clinical trial process at a meaningfully higher success rate than industry baselines. Faster, cheaper candidate generation only pays off for its pharma partners if the resulting molecules perform better downstream — and that verdict takes years, not funding rounds, to arrive.
